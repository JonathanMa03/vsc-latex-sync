\section{Introduction to Statistical Learning}

Let $(X,Y)\sim P$, where $X\in\mathcal X\subseteq\R^p$ is a feature vector
and $Y$ is a response. Statistical learning uses a sample
$\mathcal D_n=\{(X_i,Y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ to construct a prediction rule
$f:\mathcal X\to\mathcal A$. In regression, $\mathcal A=\R$; in
classification, $\mathcal A$ is a finite set or a vector of class
probabilities.

Prediction and interpretation are different goals. A predictive model is
judged on new observations from the deployment distribution. A coefficient
can be interpreted causally only with additional identification assumptions;
predictive accuracy alone does not provide them.

\begin{example}[From coin flips to supervised learning]
Suppose a coin's probability of heads depends on measurable conditions $X$,
and $Y\in\{0,1\}$ records the outcome. Estimating one bias $p$ becomes
estimating the conditional probability
\[
    \eta(x)=P(Y=1\mid X=x).
\]
A classifier converts $\eta(x)$ into an action, while a calibrated probability
model attempts to estimate $\eta(x)$ itself.
\end{example}

\subsection{Loss Functions}

A loss $L(y,a)$ quantifies the cost of predicting $a$ when the outcome is
$y$. Common choices are
\begin{align*}
 L_{\mathrm{sq}}(y,a)&=(y-a)^2,&
 L_{\mathrm{abs}}(y,a)&=\abs{y-a},\\
 L_{01}(y,a)&=\ind\{y\neq a\},&
 L_{\mathrm{log}}(y,q)&=-y\log q-(1-y)\log(1-q).
\end{align*}
Squared loss strongly penalizes large residuals; absolute loss is more robust.
Zero-one loss targets classification accuracy but is discontinuous and hard
to optimize. Logistic loss is a smooth convex surrogate and estimates class
probabilities when the model is well specified.

A loss should reflect the decision. For asymmetric binary costs $c_{10}$ for
a false positive and $c_{01}$ for a false negative,
\[
 L(y,a)=c_{10}\ind\{y=0,a=1\}+c_{01}\ind\{y=1,a=0\}.
\]

\subsection{Risk and Empirical Risk Minimization}

The population and empirical risks are
\begin{equation}
 R(f)=\E[L(Y,f(X))],
 \qquad
 \widehat R_n(f)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nL(Y_i,f(X_i)).
 \label{eq:learning-risk}
\end{equation}
Given a function class $\mathcal F$, empirical risk minimization (ERM) chooses
$\widehat f\in\argmin_{f\in\mathcal F}\widehat R_n(f)$. If $f^*$ minimizes
risk over all measurable rules, then
\[
 R(\widehat f)-R(f^*)=
 \underbrace{R(\widehat f)-\inf_{f\in\mathcal F}R(f)}_{\text{estimation error}}
 +\underbrace{\inf_{f\in\mathcal F}R(f)-R(f^*)}_{\text{approximation error}}.
\]
The first term reflects finite data; the second reflects the restricted model
class. Interpolation of training data alone gives no guarantee of
generalization.

Write $\widehat f_{\mathcal D}$ when emphasizing dependence on the training
sample. Population risk $R(g)$ evaluates a fixed rule $g$, whereas
$R(\widehat f_{\mathcal D})$ is random before $\mathcal D$ is drawn.

\begin{example}[The optimal constant rule]
Without predictors and under squared loss,
\[
 R(a)=\E[(Y-a)^2]=\Var(Y)+\{\E(Y)-a\}^2,
 \qquad
 \widehat R_{\mathcal D}(a)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(Y_i-a)^2.
\]
The Bayes rule is $a^*=\mu=\E Y$, the ERM is
$\widehat a_{\mathcal D}=\bar Y$, and the minimum population risk is
$\Var(Y)$. For $\widehat\mu_\alpha=\alpha\bar Y$,
\[
 \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat\mu_\alpha)
 =\frac{\alpha^2\sigma^2}{n}+(\alpha-1)^2\mu^2.
\]
Differentiation gives the oracle shrinkage
$\alpha^*=\mu^2/(\mu^2+\sigma^2/n)$. Although unavailable without knowing
$\mu$ and $\sigma^2$, it demonstrates how bias can reduce MSE.

More explicitly,
\[
 \operatorname{Bias}_{\mathcal D}(\widehat\mu_\alpha)
 =\E_{\mathcal D}[\widehat\mu_\alpha]-\mu
 =(\alpha-1)\mu,
 \qquad
 \Var_{\mathcal D}(\widehat\mu_\alpha)
 =\frac{\alpha^2\sigma^2}{n},
\]
so the preceding MSE is variance plus squared bias. Substitution of
$\alpha^*$ gives the minimum oracle MSE
\[
 \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat\mu_{\alpha^*})
 =\frac{\mu^2\sigma^2}{n\mu^2+\sigma^2}.
\]

Now let $Y_0$ be a new response, independent of the training sample and drawn
from the same population. Conditional on the fitted value
$\widehat\mu_\alpha$, its generalization error is
\begin{align*}
 R_{\mathcal D}(\widehat\mu_\alpha)
 &=\E\bigl[(Y_0-\widehat\mu_\alpha)^2\mid\mathcal D\bigr]\\
 &=\sigma^2+(\mu-\widehat\mu_\alpha)^2.
\end{align*}
Averaging over repeated training samples yields
\[
 \E_{\mathcal D}R_{\mathcal D}(\widehat\mu_\alpha)
 =\underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{irreducible error}}
 +\underbrace{\operatorname{MSE}(\widehat\mu_\alpha)}_{\text{estimation error}}.
\]
Thus estimation MSE and expected prediction error are not identical: predicting
a fresh response adds its irreducible variance $\sigma^2$.
\end{example}

\subsection{Optimal Prediction}

Conditioning on $X=x$ reduces prediction to a pointwise decision:
\begin{equation}
    f^*(x)\in\argmin_a\E[L(Y,a)\mid X=x].
    \label{eq:bayes-predictor}
\end{equation}
Under squared loss, $f^*(x)=\E[Y\mid X=x]$; under absolute loss, any
conditional median is optimal. Under zero-one loss, the optimal classifier
chooses the most probable class. More precisely, if
$Y\in\{1,\ldots,K\}$ and $\eta_k(x)=P(Y=k\mid X=x)$, then
\[
 g^*(x)\in\argmax_{1\leq k\leq K}\eta_k(x),
 \qquad
 \min_a\E[\ind\{Y\neq a\}\mid X=x]
 =1-\max_{1\leq k\leq K}\eta_k(x).
\]
Thus regression and classification share the same conditional-risk framework;
changing the response space and loss changes the resulting Bayes rule.

\begin{proof}[Squared-loss calculation]
Writing $m(x)=\E[Y\mid X=x]$,
\[
 \E[(Y-a)^2\mid X=x]
 =\Var(Y\mid X=x)+\{m(x)-a\}^2,
\]
which is minimized by $a=m(x)$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The Regression Function}

The regression function $m(x)=\E[Y\mid X=x]$ describes the systematic part
of a response. Writing
\[
    Y=m(X)+\varepsilon,\qquad \E[\varepsilon\mid X]=0,
\]
does not require normality or constant variance. The conditional variance
$\sigma^2(x)=\Var(Y\mid X=x)$ may depend on $x$. For Bernoulli $Y$,
$m(x)=P(Y=1\mid X=x)$ is both the regression function and the class
probability.

For any rule $g$, squared-loss risk decomposes as
\begin{equation}
 R(g)=\E[\Var(Y\mid X)]
     +\E[\{m(X)-g(X)\}^2].
 \label{eq:bayes-risk-decomposition}
\end{equation}
The first term is irreducible Bayes error; the second is reducible error.

\subsection{Nearest-Neighbor Regression and the Curse of Dimensionality}

For continuous $X$, exact replicates at $X=x$ are unlikely. The
$k$-nearest-neighbor estimator averages locally:
\[
 \widehat f_{k,\mathcal D}(x)
 =\frac1k\sum_{i:X_i\in N_k(x)}Y_i.
\]
Small $k$ tends toward low smoothing bias and high variance; large $k$ lowers
variance at the price of bias. Scaling determines the meaning of distance.

To capture fraction $r$ of a uniform distribution in the unit $p$-cube, a
smaller cube needs side length
\begin{equation}
    \ell=r^{1/p}.
    \label{eq:curse-dimensionality}
\end{equation}
For $r=0.1$, $\ell$ is approximately $0.32$ in dimension two, $0.63$ in
dimension five, and $0.79$ in dimension ten. A neighborhood containing enough
observations is no longer local. Structured model classes overcome this curse
by sharing information across feature space, but introduce approximation bias.

The same phenomenon appears in a Euclidean ball. If $X$ is uniform in the
unit ball in $\R^p$, scaling of volume gives
\[
 P(\lVert X\rVert_2\leq r)=r^p,
 \qquad 0\leq r\leq1.
\]
Consequently, its median radius is $2^{-1/p}$, which approaches $1$ as
$p\to\infty$. Thus half the volume lies outside a radius increasingly close to
the boundary. In high dimensions, even a neighborhood containing a moderate
fraction of the population must extend almost across the entire support.

\subsection{Structured Function Classes}
When the data are too sparse for reliable local averaging, learning requires
assumptions that share information across input values. For a scalar feature,
a degree-$q$ polynomial class is
\[
 \mathcal F_q
 =\left\{x\mapsto\beta_0+\beta_1x+\cdots+\beta_qx^q:
 (\beta_0,\ldots,\beta_q)\in\R^{q+1}\right\}.
\]
These classes are nested:
\[
 \mathcal F_1\subseteq\mathcal F_2\subseteq\cdots.
\]
Therefore their best empirical risks satisfy
\[
 \inf_{f\in\mathcal F_{q+1}}\widehat R_n(f)
 \leq
 \inf_{f\in\mathcal F_q}\widehat R_n(f).
\]
Enlarging a model class can never worsen its optimized training fit, although
it may worsen generalization error.

Linear and polynomial models are parametric: a finite coefficient vector fixes
their functional form. Nearest-neighbor averaging is local and nonparametric:
its effective complexity grows with the sample and it does not impose one
global formula. More structure typically improves stability and
interpretability but can increase approximation bias; less structure offers
greater flexibility at the price of higher variance and weaker
interpretability.

\subsection{Generalization Error}

Conditional on the training data, test error is
\[
 \operatorname{Err}(\mathcal D_n)
 =\E[L(Y^{\mathrm{new}},\widehat f(X^{\mathrm{new}}))\mid\mathcal D_n].
\]
Training error reuses observations that selected $\widehat f$ and is
optimistically biased. Test observations must be excluded from every modeling
choice, including imputation, scaling, feature selection, and tuning.

Ordinary validation assumes that training and deployment observations have
the same distribution. Covariate shift changes $P_X$ but not $P_{Y\mid X}$;
concept drift changes the conditional law. Random cross-validation on
historical data does not diagnose either automatically.

For an independent test pair $(X_0,Y_0)$, the precise target is
\begin{equation}
 R(\widehat f_{\mathcal D})
 =\E[(Y_0-\widehat f_{\mathcal D}(X_0))^2\mid\mathcal D].
 \label{eq:fitted-generalization-risk}
\end{equation}
Training MSE reuses the observations that selected the rule and is typically
optimistic. For nested classes, increasing flexibility cannot increase
training error, but test error can increase through greater sampling
variability.

\subsection{The Bias-Variance Tradeoff}

For squared-error prediction at fixed $x$, averaging over training sets gives
\begin{align}
 \E[(Y_0-\widehat f(x))^2\mid X_0=x]
 &=\sigma^2(x)
 +\{\E[\widehat f(x)]-m(x)\}^2
 +\Var(\widehat f(x)).
 \label{eq:prediction-bias-variance}
\end{align}
These are irreducible noise, squared bias, and variance. Restrictive models
tend toward higher bias and lower variance; flexible models tend toward lower
approximation bias and higher sampling variance. This exact additive
decomposition is specific to squared loss.

With training-set randomness shown explicitly,
\[
 \operatorname{Bias}_{\mathcal D}\{\widehat f_{\mathcal D}(x)\}
 =\E_{\mathcal D}[\widehat f_{\mathcal D}(x)]-m(x).
\]
Averaging over a new predictor gives
\begin{align*}
 \E_{\mathcal D}R(\widehat f_{\mathcal D})
 &=\E_X[\Var(Y\mid X)]
 +\E_X[\operatorname{Bias}_{\mathcal D}
       \{\widehat f_{\mathcal D}(X)\}^2]\\
 &\quad+\E_X[\Var_{\mathcal D}
       \{\widehat f_{\mathcal D}(X)\}].
\end{align*}
Training error is not a term in this decomposition and can fall below Bayes
error, which bounds new-observation risk rather than error on reused data.

\section{Least Squares}

Given $X\in\R^{n\times p}$ and $y\in\R^n$, ordinary least squares solves
\begin{equation}
    \widehat\beta\in\argmin_{\beta\in\R^p}\norm{y-X\beta}^2.
    \label{eq:ols-objective}
\end{equation}
The normal equations are
$X^\mathsf TX\widehat\beta=X^\mathsf Ty$. If $X$ has full column rank,
\[
    \widehat\beta=(X^\mathsf TX)^{-1}X^\mathsf Ty.
\]
Computationally, solve the system using QR or SVD rather than explicitly
forming the inverse; forming the Gram matrix squares the condition number.

The derivation follows by expanding
\[
 \operatorname{RSS}(\beta)
 =y^\mathsf Ty-2\beta^\mathsf TX^\mathsf Ty
  +\beta^\mathsf TX^\mathsf TX\beta.
\]
Therefore
\[
 \grad_\beta\operatorname{RSS}(\beta)
 =-2X^\mathsf Ty+2X^\mathsf TX\beta,
 \qquad
 \Hess_\beta\operatorname{RSS}=2X^\mathsf TX\succeq0.
\]
Setting the gradient to zero gives the normal equations, and positive
semidefiniteness shows that every solution is a global minimizer.

The best population linear predictor and its ERM estimate are distinct:
\[
 \beta^*=\argmin_\beta\E[(Y-X^\mathsf T\beta)^2],
 \qquad
 \widehat\beta_{\mathcal D}
 =\argmin_\beta\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n
 (Y_i-X_i^\mathsf T\beta)^2.
\]
Even when $m(x)$ is nonlinear, $x^\mathsf T\beta^*$ is the best linear
approximation under the population distribution.

To derive the population coefficient, expand
\begin{align*}
 \E[(Y-X^\mathsf T\beta)^2]
 &=\E[Y^2]-2\beta^\mathsf T\E[XY]
   +\beta^\mathsf T\E[XX^\mathsf T]\beta.
\end{align*}
Differentiating gives the population normal equations
\[
 \E[XX^\mathsf T]\beta^*=\E[XY].
\]
If $\E[XX^\mathsf T]$ is invertible, then
\begin{equation}
 \beta^*=\E[XX^\mathsf T]^{-1}\E[XY].
 \label{eq:population-linear-projection}
\end{equation}
In contrast, the empirical normal equations divide by $n$ to give
\[
 \left(\frac1nX^\mathsf TX\right)\widehat\beta
 =\frac1nX^\mathsf Ty,
\]
and hence
\[
 \widehat\beta
 =\left(\frac1nX^\mathsf TX\right)^{-1}
   \left(\frac1nX^\mathsf Ty\right).
\]
Thus OLS is the plug-in analogue of the best population linear predictor:
the empirical second moment and cross-moment replace their population
counterparts.

\subsection{Estimation and Geometry}

The fitted vector is the orthogonal projection of $y$ onto the column space:
\[
 \widehat y=Hy,\qquad
 H=X(X^\mathsf TX)^{-1}X^\mathsf T.
\]
The hat matrix is symmetric and idempotent. The residual $e=y-\widehat y$ is
orthogonal to every column of $X$. If $X$ is rank deficient, fitted values
remain unique but coefficients do not; $X^+y$ is the minimum-norm solution.

Leverage $h_{ii}=H_{ii}$ measures how unusual row $i$ is in feature space.
Since $\sum_i h_{ii}=\tr(H)=p$, average leverage is $p/n$. The leave-one-out
residual satisfies
\begin{equation}
    y_i-\widehat y_i^{(-i)}=\frac{e_i}{1-h_{ii}},
    \label{eq:loo-ols}
\end{equation}
so OLS leave-one-out error can be computed without $n$ refits.

The normal equations are equivalently $X^\mathsf Te=0$, so
$e\perp\operatorname{Col}(X)$ and $y=\widehat y+e$ is an orthogonal
decomposition. Adding a column to $X$ enlarges the projection space;
therefore RSS and training error cannot increase.

\subsection{Sampling Properties of Least Squares}

Under the fixed-design model
\[
 y=X\beta+\varepsilon,\qquad
 \E[\varepsilon\mid X]=0,\qquad
 \Cov(\varepsilon\mid X)=\sigma^2I,
\]
OLS is conditionally unbiased and
\[
    \Cov(\widehat\beta\mid X)=\sigma^2(X^\mathsf TX)^{-1}.
\]
The derivation is
\begin{align*}
 \widehat\beta
 &=(X^\mathsf TX)^{-1}X^\mathsf T(X\beta+\varepsilon)\\
 &=\beta+(X^\mathsf TX)^{-1}X^\mathsf T\varepsilon,
\end{align*}
so conditional expectation gives unbiasedness and
\begin{align*}
 \Cov(\widehat\beta\mid X)
 &=(X^\mathsf TX)^{-1}X^\mathsf T(\sigma^2I)
   X(X^\mathsf TX)^{-1}\\
 &=\sigma^2(X^\mathsf TX)^{-1}.
\end{align*}
For simple regression with an intercept,
\[
 \Var(\widehat\beta_1\mid X)
 =\frac{\sigma^2}{\sum_i(X_i-\bar X)^2}.
\]
The slope is estimated more precisely with lower noise, more observations, or
greater predictor spread.
Gauss--Markov says OLS has smallest covariance among linear unbiased
estimators; it does not require Gaussian errors. Normality gives exact $t$ and
$F$ inference. An unbiased error-variance estimator is
\[
    \widehat\sigma^2=\frac{\norm{y-X\widehat\beta}^2}{n-p}.
\]
With heteroskedastic errors, use sandwich standard errors or weighted least
squares. If $\E[\varepsilon\mid X]\neq0$, robust standard errors do not remove
coefficient bias.

\begin{example}[Omitted-variable bias]
If $Y=\beta_0+\beta_1X+\beta_2Z+\varepsilon$ but $Z$ is omitted, the population
slope from regressing $Y$ on $X$ is
\[
    \beta_1+\beta_2\frac{\Cov(X,Z)}{\Var(X)}.
\]
Bias requires both a nonzero effect of $Z$ and association between $X$ and
$Z$.
\end{example}

\subsection{Confidence and Prediction Intervals}

Let $d$ be the number of fitted columns of $X$. Under conditionally Gaussian
errors,
\[
 \frac{\widehat\beta_j-\beta_j}
 {\widehat\sigma\sqrt{[(X^\mathsf TX)^{-1}]_{jj}}}
 \sim t_{n-d}.
\]
Thus a $(1-\alpha)$ confidence interval for $\beta_j$ is
\[
 \widehat\beta_j\pm t_{n-d,1-\alpha/2}
 \widehat\sigma\sqrt{[(X^\mathsf TX)^{-1}]_{jj}}.
\]
Across repetitions of the sampling procedure, the stated fraction of such
intervals cover the fixed coefficient.

At a new feature vector $x_0$,
\[
 \Var\{x_0^\mathsf T\widehat\beta\mid X\}
 =\sigma^2x_0^\mathsf T(X^\mathsf TX)^{-1}x_0.
\]
A confidence interval for the mean response $x_0^\mathsf T\beta$ uses this
variance. A prediction interval for
$Y_0=x_0^\mathsf T\beta+\varepsilon_0$ uses
\begin{equation}
 \widehat\sigma
 \sqrt{1+x_0^\mathsf T(X^\mathsf TX)^{-1}x_0}.
 \label{eq:linear-prediction-se}
\end{equation}
It is wider because it includes irreducible noise in the new response.

\subsection{Generalization Error and the Cost of Dimension}

At fixed $x_0$, unbiasedness gives
\[
 \E_{\mathcal D,Y_0}
 [(Y_0-x_0^\mathsf T\widehat\beta)^2\mid X]
 =\sigma^2+\sigma^2x_0^\mathsf T
   (X^\mathsf TX)^{-1}x_0.
\]
Let $\Sigma_X=\E[X_0X_0^\mathsf T]$ and suppose
$n^{-1}X^\mathsf TX\approx\Sigma_X$. Averaging over $X_0$ gives
\[
 \E_{X_0}[X_0^\mathsf T(X^\mathsf TX)^{-1}X_0]
 \approx\frac1n\tr(\Sigma_X^{-1}\Sigma_X)=\frac dn.
\]
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
 \E[\text{generalization error}]
 \approx\sigma^2\left(1+\frac dn\right),
 \qquad
 \E[\text{training error}]
 =\sigma^2\left(1-\frac dn\right).
 \label{eq:linear-optimism}
\end{equation}
The expected optimism is approximately $2\sigma^2d/n$. Under correct linear
specification, added dimensions cost estimation variance; under
misspecification, they may also reduce approximation bias.

For a fixed design, the corresponding training-error calculation is exact.
Let
\[
 H=X(X^\mathsf TX)^{-1}X^\mathsf T,
 \qquad e=(I-H)y.
\]
Under the correctly specified model $y=X\beta+\varepsilon$, the identity
$(I-H)X=0$ gives $e=(I-H)\varepsilon$. Since $I-H$ is symmetric and
idempotent,
\begin{align*}
 \E[\lVert e\rVert_2^2\mid X]
 &=\E[\varepsilon^\mathsf T(I-H)\varepsilon\mid X]\\
 &=\sigma^2\tr(I-H)
 =\sigma^2(n-d).
\end{align*}
Therefore
\[
 \E\left[\frac1n\lVert y-X\widehat\beta\rVert_2^2
 \middle|X\right]
 =\sigma^2\left(1-\frac dn\right).
\]

There is also an exact fixed-design version of the test-error calculation.
At row $x_i^\mathsf T$ of $X$, a new independent response has expected squared
prediction error
\[
 \sigma^2+\sigma^2x_i^\mathsf T
 (X^\mathsf TX)^{-1}x_i
 =\sigma^2(1+h_{ii}).
\]
Averaging over the $n$ observed predictor rows and using
$\sum_i h_{ii}=\tr(H)=d$ yields
\[
 \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n
 \E[(Y_i^{\mathrm{new}}-x_i^\mathsf T\widehat\beta)^2\mid X]
 =\sigma^2\left(1+\frac dn\right).
\]
The gap between new-response and training error is consequently exactly
$2\sigma^2d/n$ for this fixed-design comparison.

\section{Multiple Regression}

An intercept, numeric predictors, and encoded categorical variables are
columns of a design matrix. With an intercept, a $K$-level categorical
predictor uses $K-1$ reference indicators. The coefficient of $X_j$ is the
change in fitted response per unit change in $X_j$, holding the other
represented features fixed.

The Frisch--Waugh--Lovell theorem says the coefficient of $X_j$ equals the
slope obtained by residualizing both $Y$ and $X_j$ against the remaining
predictors. Multiple regression therefore compares variation in $X_j$ not
linearly explained by the other columns.

To see this algebraically, partition the design as $X=[x_1\;X_2]$ and let
\[
 P_2=X_2(X_2^\mathsf TX_2)^{-1}X_2^\mathsf T,
 \qquad M_2=I-P_2.
\]
For any fixed $\beta_1$, minimizing over the coefficients of $X_2$ projects
$y-x_1\beta_1$ onto $\operatorname{Col}(X_2)$. The remaining objective is
therefore
\[
 \lVert M_2(y-x_1\beta_1)\rVert_2^2.
\]
Differentiating with respect to $\beta_1$ gives
\begin{equation}
 \widehat\beta_1
 =\frac{x_1^\mathsf TM_2y}{x_1^\mathsf TM_2x_1}
 =\frac{(M_2x_1)^\mathsf T(M_2y)}
        {(M_2x_1)^\mathsf T(M_2x_1)}.
 \label{eq:fwl-residualized-slope}
\end{equation}
Thus $\widehat\beta_1$ is the simple-regression slope between the part of
$x_1$ unexplained by $X_2$ and the part of $y$ unexplained by $X_2$. If
$x_1$ is orthogonal to every column of $X_2$, then $M_2x_1=x_1$, so adding
$X_2$ does not change its coefficient.

\subsection{Interactions and Basis Expansions}

An interaction permits one effect to depend on another:
\[
    m(x_1,x_2)=\beta_0+\beta_1x_1+\beta_2x_2+\beta_{12}x_1x_2.
\]
Then $\partial m/\partial x_1=\beta_1+\beta_{12}x_2$, so $\beta_1$ is the
effect of $x_1$ only at $x_2=0$. Centering can improve interpretation.

For a binary group indicator $Z\in\{0,1\}$, the additive model
\[
 m(x,z)=\beta_0+\beta_1x+\beta_2z
\]
fits parallel lines: group $0$ has intercept $\beta_0$, group $1$ has
intercept $\beta_0+\beta_2$, and both have slope $\beta_1$. Adding an
interaction,
\[
 m(x,z)=\beta_0+\beta_1x+\beta_2z+\beta_3xz,
\]
allows group $1$ to have slope $\beta_1+\beta_3$. Hence $\beta_2$ is the
between-group intercept change at $x=0$, while $\beta_3$ is the
between-group slope change.

A basis expansion uses fixed transformations $h_m$:
\[
    f(x)=\sum_{m=1}^M\beta_mh_m(x).
\]
The model is nonlinear in $x$ but linear in $\beta$. Raw high-degree
polynomials are often unstable; orthogonal polynomials or splines are usually
better conditioned.

\section{Logistic Regression}

For $Y\in\{0,1\}$, logistic regression models
\begin{equation}
 p(x)=P(Y=1\mid X=x),\qquad
 \log\frac{p(x)}{1-p(x)}=\beta_0+x^\mathsf T\beta.
 \label{eq:logistic-model}
\end{equation}
Thus $p(x)=\{1+\exp[-(\beta_0+x^\mathsf T\beta)]\}^{-1}$. A unit increase in
$x_j$ multiplies conditional odds by $e^{\beta_j}$ holding other modeled
features fixed; it does not add a constant amount to probability.

\subsection{Log Loss and Probability Estimation}
When the prediction is a probability $q\in(0,1)$ rather than a class label,
the binary log loss is
\[
 L_{\log}(y,q)=-y\log q-(1-y)\log(1-q).
\]
Fix $x$ and write $p=P(Y=1\mid X=x)$. The conditional risk of reporting $q$
is
\[
 R_x(q)=-p\log q-(1-p)\log(1-q).
\]
Its derivatives are
\[
 R_x'(q)=-\frac pq+\frac{1-p}{1-q}
 =\frac{q-p}{q(1-q)},
 \qquad
 R_x''(q)=\frac{p}{q^2}+\frac{1-p}{(1-q)^2}>0.
\]
Hence the unique minimizer is
\[
 q^*(x)=p=P(Y=1\mid X=x).
\]
Thus zero--one loss targets the most likely class, whereas log loss targets the
entire conditional probability. Equivalently,
\[
 R_x(q)=H(p)+D_{\mathrm{KL}}
 \bigl(\operatorname{Bern}(p)\,\|\,\operatorname{Bern}(q)\bigr),
\]
so any incorrect probability incurs nonnegative excess conditional risk.

\subsection{Likelihood}

Including the intercept in $x_i$, the Bernoulli log-likelihood is
\begin{equation}
 \ell(\beta)=\sum_{i=1}^n
 [y_ix_i^\mathsf T\beta-\log\{1+e^{x_i^\mathsf T\beta}\}].
 \label{eq:logistic-likelihood}
\end{equation}
The negative log-likelihood is exactly the empirical log loss:
\[
 -\ell(\beta)=\sum_{i=1}^n
 L_{\log}\bigl(y_i,p_\beta(x_i)\bigr).
\]
Consequently, logistic maximum likelihood is empirical risk minimization under
log loss.
If $p_i=p(x_i)$, then
\[
 \grad\ell(\beta)=X^\mathsf T(y-p),\qquad
 \Hess\ell(\beta)=-X^\mathsf TWX,
\]
where $W=\diag\{p_i(1-p_i)\}$. The likelihood is concave.

Under perfect separation, a hyperplane correctly separates all observations
and the likelihood supremum occurs as $\norm\beta\to\infty$; no finite
unpenalized MLE exists. Ridge regularization gives a finite solution.
Numerically, evaluate $\log(1+e^z)$ with a stable softplus routine.

\subsection{Fitting Logistic Regression: Newton and IRLS}

Newton's update is
\begin{equation}
 \beta^{\mathrm{new}}
 =\beta^{\mathrm{old}}+(X^\mathsf TWX)^{-1}X^\mathsf T(y-p).
 \label{eq:logistic-newton}
\end{equation}
With working response $z=X\beta+W^{-1}(y-p)$, this is weighted least squares:
\[
    \beta^{\mathrm{new}}=(X^\mathsf TWX)^{-1}X^\mathsf TWz.
\]
This is iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS). Implementations solve the
weighted system rather than invert it, and use step halving or line search if
a full Newton step decreases the likelihood. Growing coefficients and fitted
probabilities near zero or one suggest separation.

\section{Bayes Classification}

Let $Y\in\{1,\ldots,K\}$ and $\eta_k(x)=P(Y=k\mid X=x)$. Under zero-one loss,
the Bayes classifier is
\[
    g^*(x)=\argmax_k\eta_k(x),
\]
with Bayes error $R^*=\E[1-\max_k\eta_k(X)]$.

\subsection{Plug-in Classification and kNN}
A plug-in classifier first estimates the conditional class probabilities and
then substitutes them into the Bayes rule:
\[
 \widehat g(x)\in\argmax_k\widehat\eta_k(x).
\]
For $k$-nearest-neighbor classification, let $N_k(x)$ denote the indices of
the $k$ training inputs closest to $x$. Estimate
\[
 \widehat\eta_c(x)
 =\frac1k\sum_{i\in N_k(x)}\ind\{Y_i=c\},
 \qquad c=1,\ldots,K,
\]
and predict the local majority class. Small $k$ produces a flexible boundary
with low smoothing bias and high sampling variance; large $k$ produces a
smoother, less variable boundary with greater bias.

For direct multiclass probability modeling, softmax regression uses
\[
 p_k(x)=
 \frac{\exp(\alpha_k+x^\mathsf T\beta_k)}
 {\sum_{j=1}^K\exp(\alpha_j+x^\mathsf T\beta_j)},
 \qquad k=1,\ldots,K.
\]
The probabilities are positive and sum to one. After fitting by multiclass log
loss, the corresponding zero--one-loss prediction is
$\widehat g(x)=\argmax_k\widehat p_k(x)$. One class or one coefficient vector
must be fixed as a reference because adding the same score to every class does
not change the probabilities.

Generative classifiers model priors $\pi_k$ and class densities $f_k$, then
apply
\[
 P(Y=k\mid X=x)=\frac{\pi_kf_k(x)}{\sum_j\pi_jf_j(x)}.
\]

\subsection{Linear Discriminant Analysis}
Linear discriminant analysis assumes
\[
 X\mid Y=k\sim\mathcal N(\mu_k,\Sigma),
 \qquad k=1,\ldots,K,
\]
with a common covariance matrix across classes. For classification, the
denominator in Bayes' theorem is common to all $k$, so it suffices to maximize
$\log\pi_k+\log f_k(x)$. Apart from constants common to every class,
\begin{align*}
 \log\pi_k+\log f_k(x)
 &=-\frac12(x-\mu_k)^\mathsf T\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu_k)
   +\log\pi_k+\text{constant}\\
 &=x^\mathsf T\Sigma^{-1}\mu_k
   -\frac12\mu_k^\mathsf T\Sigma^{-1}\mu_k
   +\log\pi_k
   -\frac12x^\mathsf T\Sigma^{-1}x+	ext{constant}.
\end{align*}
The final quadratic term does not depend on $k$ and cancels when classes are
compared. Define the LDA discriminant score
\begin{equation}
 \delta_k(x)=x^\mathsf T\Sigma^{-1}\mu_k
 -\frac12\mu_k^\mathsf T\Sigma^{-1}\mu_k+log\pi_k.
 \label{eq:lda-discriminant}
\end{equation}
Then
\[
 g_{\mathrm{LDA}}(x)=\argmax_k\delta_k(x).
\]
Since each $\delta_k$ is affine in $x$, the pairwise boundary
$\delta_k(x)=\delta_\ell(x)$ is a hyperplane. The class means, covariance,
and priors determine its orientation and location.

Given $n_k$ observations in class $k$, the usual fitted quantities are
\[
 \widehat\pi_k=\frac{n_k}{n},
 \qquad
 \widehat\mu_k=\frac1{n_k}\sum_{i:Y_i=k}X_i,
\]
and the pooled within-class covariance
\[
 \widehat\Sigma
 =\frac1{n-K}\sum_{k=1}^K
   \sum_{i:Y_i=k}(X_i-\widehat\mu_k)(X_i-\widehat\mu_k)^\mathsf T.
\]
Substituting these estimates into \eqref{eq:lda-discriminant} produces the
fitted LDA rule.

\subsection{Quadratic Discriminant Analysis}
Quadratic discriminant analysis instead assumes
\[
 X\mid Y=k\sim\mathcal N(\mu_k,\Sigma_k).
\]
Its discriminant score is
\begin{equation}
 \delta_k^{\mathrm{QDA}}(x)
 =-\frac12\log|\Sigma_k|
  -\frac12(x-\mu_k)^\mathsf T\Sigma_k^{-1}(x-\mu_k)
  +\log\pi_k.
 \label{eq:qda-discriminant}
\end{equation}
Because the covariance differs by class, the terms
$x^\mathsf T\Sigma_k^{-1}x$ no longer cancel. Pairwise decision boundaries
are therefore quadratic.

LDA estimates one covariance matrix with $p(p+1)/2$ free entries, whereas QDA
estimates $K$ such matrices. QDA can reduce bias when class covariances truly
differ, but its greater parameter count increases variance and requires more
data. LDA imposes more structure and is typically more stable.

For two classes, the posterior log-odds make the relationship to logistic
regression explicit:
\[
 \log\frac{P(Y=1\mid X=x)}{P(Y=0\mid X=x)}
 =\log\frac{\pi_1f_1(x)}{\pi_0f_0(x)}.
\]
Shared covariance makes this expression affine in $x$; distinct covariances
make it quadratic. Logistic regression models the posterior log-odds directly,
whereas LDA and QDA obtain them from a model for $X\mid Y$.

\subsection{Naive Bayes}
Naive Bayes assumes the feature coordinates are conditionally independent
within each class:
\[
 f_k(x)=p(X=x\mid Y=k)
 =\prod_{j=1}^p f_{kj}(x_j).
\]
The resulting discriminant score can be written
\[
 \log\pi_k+\sum_{j=1}^p\log f_{kj}(x_j).
\]
Each marginal $f_{kj}$ may be modeled according to its feature type, for
example Gaussian, Bernoulli, Poisson, or nonparametrically. Gaussian naive
Bayes corresponds to class-specific diagonal covariance matrices. It uses far
fewer covariance parameters than QDA, at the cost of the strong conditional
independence assumption.

\subsection{Multiclass Decisions under a Loss Matrix}
Estimating class probabilities and choosing an action are distinct steps. Let
$L(j,k)$ be the loss for predicting class $j$ when the true class is $k$.
At a fixed $x$, the conditional risk of action $j$ is
\[
 R_x(j)=\sum_{k=1}^K L(j,k)\eta_k(x),
 \qquad \eta_k(x)=P(Y=k\mid X=x).
\]
The Bayes action is
\begin{equation}
 g_L^*(x)\in\argmin_{1\leq j\leq K}R_x(j).
 \label{eq:multiclass-loss-rule}
\end{equation}
Only under zero--one loss does this reduce to choosing the most probable class.

For example, suppose
\[
 \eta(x)=\begin{pmatrix}0.45\\0.35\\0.20\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad
 L=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&5\\1&0&1\\5&1&0\end{pmatrix},
\]
where rows are predictions and columns are true classes. Then
\[
 R_x(1)=1.35,\qquad R_x(2)=0.65,\qquad R_x(3)=2.60.
\]
Class $1$ is most probable, but class $2$ minimizes expected loss because it
avoids the costly confusion between classes $1$ and $3$.

\subsection{Thresholds}

With false-positive cost $c_{10}$ and false-negative cost $c_{01}$, predict
class $1$ when
\begin{equation}
    \eta(x)>\frac{c_{10}}{c_{10}+c_{01}}.
    \label{eq:cost-threshold}
\end{equation}
Indeed, fixing $x$ and writing $\eta(x)=P(Y=1\mid X=x)$, the two conditional
risks are
\[
 R_x(1)=c_{10}\{1-\eta(x)\},\qquad
 R_x(0)=c_{01}\eta(x).
\]
The inequality $R_x(1)<R_x(0)$ rearranges to
$\eta(x)>c_{10}/(c_{10}+c_{01})$, which proves
\cref{eq:cost-threshold}. Thus a threshold is not an arbitrary convention: it
encodes the relative consequences of the two errors.
The threshold $1/2$ is optimal only for equal costs. Changing a threshold
changes decisions, not ranking or calibration. Select it on validation data
using deployment costs or capacity constraints.

For a threshold rule $g_t(x)=\ind\{\widehat\eta(x)>t\}$, let
$\pi_1=P(Y=1)$ and $\pi_0=P(Y=0)$. Its population cost can be written in terms
of its ROC operating point:
\begin{align}
 R(g_t)
 &=c_{01}P(g_t(X)=0,Y=1)+c_{10}P(g_t(X)=1,Y=0)\notag\\
 &=c_{01}\pi_1\{1-\operatorname{TPR}(t)\}
   +c_{10}\pi_0\operatorname{FPR}(t).
 \label{eq:roc-operating-risk}
\end{align}
Thus the preferred ROC operating point depends on error costs and class
prevalence; the curve alone does not select a deployment threshold.

\subsection{ROC and Calibration}

For binary classification, the confusion matrix records true positives
($\mathrm{TP}$), false positives ($\mathrm{FP}$), true negatives
($\mathrm{TN}$), and false negatives ($\mathrm{FN}$). The principal rates are
\[
 \operatorname{TPR}=\frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN}},\qquad
 \operatorname{FPR}=\frac{\mathrm{FP}}{\mathrm{FP}+\mathrm{TN}},\qquad
 \operatorname{specificity}=1-\operatorname{FPR}.
\]
TPR is also called sensitivity or recall. These are conditional rates and
should not be confused with precision
$\mathrm{TP}/(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP})$, which depends directly on class
prevalence.

For threshold $t$,
\[
 \operatorname{TPR}(t)=P(\widehat\eta(X)>t\mid Y=1),\qquad
 \operatorname{FPR}(t)=P(\widehat\eta(X)>t\mid Y=0).
\]
The ROC curve plots TPR against FPR. AUC is the probability that a random
positive receives a higher score than a random negative, with half credit for
ties. It assesses ranking, not probability accuracy, and may hide behavior in
the operational low-FPR region.

To see the ranking interpretation, let $S^+$ and $S^-$ be independent scores
drawn from the positive and negative classes, and let $F_-$ be the cdf of
$S^-$. For continuous scores,
\begin{align*}
 \operatorname{AUC}
 &=\int \operatorname{TPR}(t)\,dF_-(t)\\
 &=\int P(S^+>t)\,dF_-(t)
 =\E\{P(S^+>S^-\mid S^-)\}\\
 &=P(S^+>S^-).
\end{align*}
This also shows why any strictly increasing transformation of the scores
leaves the ROC curve and AUC unchanged.
When scores are discrete, ties may have positive probability and the precise
pairwise formula is
\[
 \operatorname{AUC}=P(S^+>S^-)+\frac12P(S^+=S^-).
\]
Consequently a score independent of the class has AUC $1/2$, whether the score
distribution is continuous or discrete.

For multiclass evaluation, one-versus-rest calculations produce a metric for
each class. A \emph{macro average} gives every class equal weight, for example
\[
 \operatorname{TPR}_{\mathrm{macro}}
 =\frac1K\sum_{k=1}^K\operatorname{TPR}_k.
\]
A \emph{micro average} first pools all one-versus-rest counts:
\[
 \operatorname{TPR}_{\mathrm{micro}}
 =\frac{\sum_k\operatorname{TP}_k}
        {\sum_k(\operatorname{TP}_k+\operatorname{FN}_k)}.
\]
Macro averaging exposes performance on rare classes, while micro averaging is
dominated by common classes.

A score is calibrated when
$P(Y=1\mid\widehat\eta(X)=q)=q$. Reliability plots, log loss, and Brier score
assess calibration. Platt scaling fits a logistic recalibration; isotonic
regression fits a monotone map. Under class imbalance, precision--recall
curves are often more informative because precision depends on prevalence.

\section{Estimating Generalization Error}

The quantity of interest is performance of the entire training procedure,
including tuning. A test set estimates this only if it is untouched until the
procedure is finalized. Repeatedly checking test performance turns the test
set into training information.

Let $D=\{(X_i,Y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ and let $\widehat f_D$ denote the predictor
produced by a learning procedure. Its training error is
\[
 \widehat R_D(\widehat f_D)
 =\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n L\{Y_i,\widehat f_D(X_i)\},
\]
whereas, conditional on the realized training set, its generalization error is
\begin{equation}
 R(\widehat f_D)
 =\E\!\left[L\{Y,\widehat f_D(X)\}\mid D\right],
 \label{eq:conditional-generalization-error}
\end{equation}
for a fresh $(X,Y)$ from the population. Training error is generally
optimistic because the same observations were used both to choose
$\widehat f_D$ and to assess it.

An independent test set $T$ gives the conditional estimate
\[
 \widehat R_T(\widehat f_D)
 =\frac1{|T|}\sum_{(X_i,Y_i)\in T}L\{Y_i,\widehat f_D(X_i)\}.
\]
Once test performance is used to choose among procedures, however, the chosen
index $\widehat m=\argmin_m\widehat R_T(\widehat f_D^{(m)})$ depends on $T$.
Consequently $\widehat R_T(\widehat f_D^{(\widehat m)})$ is optimistic as a
final assessment. The three distinct roles are therefore: training data fit
parameters, validation data select the procedure, and untouched test data
assess the finalized procedure.

\subsection{Validation and CV}

In a validation split, train on one subset, choose hyperparameters on another,
and evaluate once on a test subset. In $K$-fold cross-validation (CV), partition
indices into folds $I_1,\ldots,I_K$ and compute
\[
 \widehat{\operatorname{Err}}_{\mathrm{CV}}
 =\frac1n\sum_{k=1}^K\sum_{i\in I_k}
 L\{Y_i,\widehat f^{(-k)}(X_i)\}.
\]
Five- or ten-fold CV is a common variance--computation compromise. Leave-one-
out CV has nearly maximal training sets but can have high variance and is not
always preferable.

More explicitly, leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) fits
$\widehat f_{D_{-i}}$ on $D_{-i}=D\setminus\{(X_i,Y_i)\}$ and reports
\begin{equation}
 \widehat R_{\mathrm{LOO}}
 =\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n
 L\{Y_i,\widehat f_{D_{-i}}(X_i)\}.
 \label{eq:loocv-error}
\end{equation}
Each response is assessed by a predictor that did not use it. Thus LOOCV
targets the expected generalization error of the \emph{learning procedure}
trained on $n-1$ observations, rather than the conditional error of one fixed
fitted model. The distinction from training on all $n$ observations is often
small for stable procedures and large $n$, but it is conceptually important.
For least squares, substituting \cref{eq:loo-ols} into squared-error loss gives
\[
 \widehat R_{\mathrm{LOO}}
 =\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\left(\frac{e_i}{1-h_{ii}}\right)^2,
\]
so one full-data fit suffices.

Preprocessing must be refit within each training fold. For grouped,
longitudinal, spatial, or time-ordered data, folds must respect dependence and
deployment direction. Random row splits can leak subjects or future
information.

``Preprocessing'' includes every data-dependent operation: feature screening,
standardization, imputation, transformations, dimensionality reduction, and
model fitting. For example, selecting the predictors most correlated with
$Y$ using the full dataset before CV leaks validation responses into the
training process. The selection must instead be repeated separately inside
each training fold.

Nested CV uses inner folds for tuning and outer folds for evaluation. The
one-standard-error rule chooses the simplest model whose estimated error is
within one standard error of the minimum, favoring stability when differences
are mostly noise.

\subsection{Bootstrap and Statistical Uncertainty}

The nonparametric bootstrap samples $n$ training observations with replacement,
refits the complete procedure, and approximates sampling variability from the
replicates. About $1-e^{-1}\approx0.632$ of distinct observations appear in a
large bootstrap sample; the rest are out-of-bag observations.

Formally, if $D=\{Z_1,\ldots,Z_n\}$ consists of i.i.d. observations from an
unknown distribution $P$, the empirical distribution is
\[
 \widehat P_D=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\delta_{Z_i},
 \qquad \widehat P_D(Z=Z_i)=\frac1n.
\]
The bootstrap substitutes $\widehat P_D$ for $P$ and draws conditionally
independent datasets
\[
 D^{*(1)},\ldots,D^{*(B)}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\widehat P_D^{,n}.
\]
Recomputing a statistic $T$ on each sample gives the bootstrap distribution
$T^{*(1)},\ldots,T^{*(B)}$, which approximates the repeated-sampling
distribution of $T(D)$. This is a conditional simulation: the observed data
are held fixed while the bootstrap randomness varies.

The $0.632$ fact follows by fixing observation $Z_i$. It is absent on one draw
with probability $1-1/n$, hence
\[
 P_*(Z_i\notin D^*)=\left(1-\frac1n\right)^n\longrightarrow e^{-1},
 \qquad
 P_*(Z_i\in D^*)\longrightarrow1-e^{-1}.
\]

Bootstrap standard errors estimate variability of parameters or predictions:
\[
 \widehat{\operatorname{se}}_{\mathrm{boot}}(\widehat\theta)
 =\left\{\frac1{B-1}\sum_{b=1}^B
 (\widehat\theta^{*(b)}-\bar\theta^*)^2\right\}^{1/2}.
\]
Percentile, basic, and BCa intervals make different corrections. The ordinary
bootstrap can fail for nonsmooth statistics, parameters on boundaries,
extreme tails, or dependent observations. Use block bootstrap for suitable
dependent sequences.

What is resampled must match the intended sampling experiment. Resampling
observed pairs $(X_i,Y_i)$ lets both the design and response vary and targets
random-design uncertainty. If regression inference is conditional on fixed
$X$, a residual bootstrap instead fits the model, centers the residuals
$e_i=Y_i-\widehat Y_i$, samples $e_i^*$ with replacement, constructs
\[
 Y_i^*=\widehat Y_i+e_i^*,
\]
and refits using the original $X_i$. Independently resampling observations
from a time series destroys serial dependence; a suitable block bootstrap
preserves local dependence. In all cases, the bootstrap should imitate the
population experiment whose uncertainty is being estimated.

\subsubsection{Bootstrap Aggregation}

Bootstrap replicates can also be averaged rather than merely inspected for
their spread. For predictions, bootstrap aggregation (\emph{bagging}) uses
\[
 \widehat f_{\mathrm{bag}}(x)
 =\frac1B\sum_{b=1}^B\widehat f_{D^{*(b)}}(x)
 \longrightarrow
 \E_*\{\widehat f_{D^*}(x)\mid D\}.
\]
This can reduce variance for unstable methods, whose fitted rules change
substantially after small perturbations of the training data.

The effect is especially transparent for 1-nearest-neighbor regression. Fix
$x_0$ and order the training points by distance as
$X_{(1)},\ldots,X_{(n)}$. Let $J$ be the rank of the first point that appears
in a size-$n$ bootstrap sample. Then
\begin{align*}
 w_j=P_*(J=j)
 &=\left(\frac{n-j+1}{n}\right)^n
   -\left(\frac{n-j}{n}\right)^n\\
 &\longrightarrow e^{-(j-1)}-e^{-j}.
\end{align*}
Therefore infinitely bagged 1-NN is the weighted-neighbor rule
\[
 \E_*\{\widehat f^{\mathrm{1NN}}_{D^*}(x_0)\mid D\}
 =\sum_{j=1}^n w_jY_{(j)}
 \approx .632Y_{(1)}+.233Y_{(2)}+.086Y_{(3)}+\cdots.
\]
Bagging has changed the learning rule from a discontinuous single-neighbor
prediction into a smoother average of nearby responses.

CV fold errors are correlated because fitted training sets overlap; treating
them as independent observations understates uncertainty. Comparing two
models by paired out-of-fold losses is better than comparing separate averages,
but repeated or nested resampling is needed for honest selection uncertainty.

\section{Model Selection}

Model selection chooses features, transformations, hyperparameters, or an
algorithm. Training fit always improves as a nested model grows, so selection
must penalize complexity or estimate out-of-sample error.

For Gaussian linear models, common criteria are
\[
 \mathrm{AIC}=-2\ell(\widehat\theta)+2d,\qquad
 \mathrm{BIC}=-2\ell(\widehat\theta)+d\log n,
\]
where $d$ is the number of fitted parameters. AIC targets predictive
Kullback--Leibler risk; BIC more strongly favors small models and, under
restrictive conditions, consistently selects a true finite-dimensional model.
They answer different questions.

Searching many models creates selection bias. Standard errors and $p$-values
computed as if the selected model had been fixed in advance are generally too
optimistic.

\subsection{Subset Selection}

For a subset $S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,p\}$, define the least-squares training
criterion
\[
 \operatorname{RSS}(S)
 =\min_{\beta_0,\{\beta_j:j\in S\}}
   \sum_{i=1}^n\left(Y_i-\beta_0-\sum_{j\in S}\beta_jX_{ij}\right)^2.
\]
Best-subset selection retains, for every size $k$, the candidate
\[
 S_k^*\in\argmin_{|S|=k}\operatorname{RSS}(S).
\]
There are $\binom pk$ candidates of size $k$ and $2^p$ subsets in total, so
exhaustive search quickly becomes infeasible. Although
$\operatorname{RSS}(S_k^*)$ cannot increase with $k$, the best subsets need
not be nested: $S_k^*$ need not be contained in $S_{k+1}^*$. Training RSS is
used to find the best candidate \emph{within} each size; CV or a complexity
criterion must still choose the size.

Forward stepwise selection starts with $S_0=\varnothing$ and greedily updates
\[
 j_k^*\in\argmin_{j\notin S_k}\operatorname{RSS}(S_k\cup\{j\}),
 \qquad S_{k+1}=S_k\cup\{j_k^*\}.
\]
Backward stepwise selection starts with all predictors and greedily removes
\[
 j_k^*\in\argmin_{j\in S_k}\operatorname{RSS}(S_k\setminus\{j\}),
 \qquad S_{k-1}=S_k\setminus\{j_k^*\}.
\]
Both produce nested paths after roughly $1+p(p+1)/2$ fits, but their greedy
paths need not contain the best subset of a given size. Backward selection
also requires that the full model be fit, so it is unavailable in ordinary
least squares when $p\geq n$. When CV evaluates either procedure, the entire
selection path must be recomputed inside each training fold.

\subsection{Complexity and Degree of Freedom}

Degrees of freedom measure how strongly fitted values respond to the observed
responses, not simply the number of nonzero parameters.

For a fixed-design Gaussian model
$Y=\mu+\varepsilon$ with
$\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon\mid X)=\sigma^2I$, the covariance definition
is
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{df}(\widehat Y)
 =\frac1{\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n
   \operatorname{Cov}(\widehat Y_i,Y_i\mid X).
 \label{eq:covariance-df}
\end{equation}
If $\widehat Y=HY$ is a linear smoother, then
\begin{align*}
 \operatorname{Cov}(\widehat Y_i,Y_i\mid X)
 &=\sum_jH_{ij}\operatorname{Cov}(Y_j,Y_i\mid X)
 =\sigma^2H_{ii},
\end{align*}
and hence $\operatorname{df}(\widehat Y)=\tr(H)$. For OLS with a fixed,
rank-$d$ design,
\[
 \tr\{X(X^\mathsf TX)^{-1}X^\mathsf T\}
 =\tr\{(X^\mathsf TX)^{-1}X^\mathsf TX\}=d.
\]

Adaptive search consumes additional degrees of freedom beyond the number of
coefficients eventually retained. For a simple example, let
$Y_1,Y_2\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}N(0,\sigma^2)$ and fit whichever coordinate
has larger absolute value:
\[
 \widehat Y=
 \begin{cases}
  (Y_1,0),&|Y_1|\geq|Y_2|,\\
  (0,Y_2),&|Y_2|>|Y_1|.
 \end{cases}
\]
Only one coordinate is retained, yet \cref{eq:covariance-df} gives
\[
 \operatorname{df}(\widehat Y)
 =\frac1{\sigma^2}\E\{\max(Y_1^2,Y_2^2)\}
 =1+\frac2\pi\approx1.64>1.
\]
The excess is the cost of using $Y$ to search for the apparently stronger
coordinate.

Mallows' $C_p$ and Stein's unbiased risk estimate correct training error by an
optimism term proportional to degrees of freedom. Complexity can also be
controlled through norm constraints, tree depth, early stopping, margin, or
algorithmic stability.

More precisely, let $Y^{\mathrm{new}}$ be an independent response vector at
the same fixed design points and define
$R_{\mathrm{train}}=n^{-1}\norm{Y-\widehat Y}^2$. Then
\begin{equation}
 \frac1n\E\norm{Y^{\mathrm{new}}-\widehat Y}^2
 =\E(R_{\mathrm{train}})
  +\frac{2\sigma^2}{n}\operatorname{df}(\widehat Y).
 \label{eq:optimism-df}
\end{equation}
For a fixed $d$-parameter least-squares model, this suggests the corrected
error estimate
\[
 \widehat R_{C_p}=\frac{\operatorname{RSS}}n+\frac{2\widehat\sigma^2d}{n},
\]
or equivalently the classical standardized form
$C_p=\operatorname{RSS}/\widehat\sigma^2-n+2d$. If variable selection is
adaptive, substituting only the selected coefficient count for $d$ ignores
search degrees of freedom and can under-penalize the procedure.

\section{Ridge Regression}

Ridge regression solves
\begin{equation}
 \widehat\beta_\lambda
 =\argmin_\beta\{\norm{y-X\beta}^2+\lambda\norm\beta^2\},
 \qquad\lambda\geq0,
 \label{eq:ridge-objective}
\end{equation}
giving
\[
    \widehat\beta_\lambda=(X^\mathsf TX+\lambda I)^{-1}X^\mathsf Ty.
\]
For centered $X$ and $y$, differentiating the objective gives
\[
 \nabla_\beta\{\norm{y-X\beta}^2+\lambda\norm\beta^2\}
 =-2X^\mathsf Ty+2(X^\mathsf TX+\lambda I)\beta,
\]
so the ridge normal equations are
$(X^\mathsf TX+\lambda I)\widehat\beta_\lambda=X^\mathsf Ty$.
For every nonzero $v$ and $\lambda>0$,
\[
 v^\mathsf T(X^\mathsf TX+\lambda I)v
 =\norm{Xv}^2+\lambda\norm v^2>0.
\]
Thus $X^\mathsf TX+\lambda I$ is positive definite and the ridge solution is
unique even when predictors are linearly dependent or $p>n$.

The intercept is normally left unpenalized. Predictors must be standardized
because the penalty depends on units. Ridge is also the posterior mode and
mean under a Gaussian prior in the Gaussian linear model.

Concretely, a common transformation is
\[
 Z_{ij}=\frac{X_{ij}-\bar X_j}{s_j},
 \qquad
 s_j^2=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(X_{ij}-\bar X_j)^2.
\]
Without it, changing a predictor from dollars to thousands of dollars changes
the corresponding squared-coefficient penalty by a factor of $10^6$.
Centering and scaling constants must be estimated within each CV training
fold and then applied unchanged to its validation fold.

\subsection{Shrinkage}

Conditional on $X$ under $y=X\beta+\varepsilon$,
\[
 \E[\widehat\beta_\lambda]
 =(X^\mathsf TX+\lambda I)^{-1}X^\mathsf TX\beta,
\]
so ridge is biased. It can nevertheless lower prediction MSE by greatly
reducing variance, especially under collinearity or $p\geq n$. Unlike lasso,
ridge usually retains every feature.

As $\lambda$ increases, coefficient norm and effective degrees of freedom
decrease. Tune $\lambda$ by validation, fitting standardization within each
fold. The constrained form $\norm\beta^2\leq t$ is equivalent for a matching
Lagrange multiplier.

\subsection{SVD}

Let the centered design have thin SVD $X=UDV^\mathsf T$, with singular values
$d_1\geq\cdots\geq d_r>0$. Then
\[
 \widehat\beta_\lambda
 =V\diag\left\{\frac{d_j}{d_j^2+\lambda}\right\}U^\mathsf Ty,
\quad
 \widehat y_\lambda
 =U\diag\left\{\frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2+\lambda}\right\}U^\mathsf Ty.
\]
Ridge shrinks low-variance directions most strongly. Its effective degrees of
freedom are
\[
    \operatorname{df}(\lambda)=\sum_{j=1}^r\frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2+\lambda}.
\]
This counts penalized slope complexity; add one when an intercept is fit but
left unpenalized.

The SVD also exposes what ridge changes relative to OLS. When $r<p$, every OLS
solution has the form
\[
 \widehat\beta_{\mathrm{OLS}}
 =VD^{-1}U^\mathsf Ty+w,
 \qquad V^\mathsf Tw=0.
\]
The two terms are orthogonal, so $w=0$ gives the unique minimum-norm OLS
solution. In singular directions,
\begin{align*}
 \widehat\beta_{\mathrm{OLS}}^{\min}
 &=\sum_{j=1}^r\frac{u_j^\mathsf Ty}{d_j}v_j,\\
 \widehat\beta_\lambda
 &=\sum_{j=1}^r\frac{d_j}{d_j^2+\lambda}(u_j^\mathsf Ty)v_j.
\end{align*}
Hence ridge multiplies the OLS coefficient in direction $v_j$ by
$d_j^2/(d_j^2+\lambda)$, shrinking weakly identified directions with small
$d_j$ most strongly.
The SVD expression explains stability, shrinkage, and why ridge remains
well-defined when $X^\mathsf TX$ is singular.

\subsection{Bias--Variance Tradeoff}

Write the fixed-design model as $y=\mu+\varepsilon$ with
$\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon\mid X)=\sigma^2I$, and define the ridge
smoother
\[
 H_\lambda=X(X^\mathsf TX+\lambda I)^{-1}X^\mathsf T
 =U\diag\left\{\frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2+\lambda}\right\}U^\mathsf T.
\]
Then
\[
 \operatorname{Bias}(\widehat y_\lambda\mid X)
 =(H_\lambda-I)\mu,
 \qquad
 \operatorname{Var}(\widehat y_\lambda\mid X)=\sigma^2H_\lambda^2.
\]
Consequently the average variance contribution to prediction error is
\begin{equation}
 \frac1n\tr\{\operatorname{Var}(\widehat y_\lambda\mid X)\}
 =\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\sum_{j=1}^r
   \left(\frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2+\lambda}\right)^2.
 \label{eq:ridge-variance}
\end{equation}
Increasing $\lambda$ decreases this variance monotonically while generally
increasing squared bias. Cross-validation estimates the value of $\lambda$
that best balances the two for prediction; the one-standard-error rule chooses
the largest penalty whose CV error is within one standard error of the minimum.

\section{Lasso Regression}

The lasso solves
\begin{equation}
 \widehat\beta_\lambda
 =\argmin_\beta\left\{\frac12\norm{y-X\beta}^2
 +\lambda\norm\beta_1\right\}.
 \label{eq:lasso-objective}
\end{equation}
It is convex but nonsmooth at zero. The $\ell_1$ constraint has corners, so
solutions often lie on coordinate axes and are sparse.

\subsection{Sparsity}

Lasso performs shrinkage and variable selection together. With correlated
predictors it may select one arbitrarily, making the selected set unstable.
Ridge tends to distribute weight across the group. Elastic net combines
$\ell_1$ and squared $\ell_2$ penalties and often stabilizes grouped features.

Subset selection can be written as an $\ell_0$-penalized problem,
\[
 \min_\beta\left\{\frac12\norm{y-X\beta}^2
 +\lambda_0\norm\beta_0\right\},
 \qquad
 \norm\beta_0=\sum_{j=1}^p\ind\{\beta_j\neq0\}.
\]
It charges the same amount for every retained predictor, regardless of its
coefficient magnitude. This objective is nonconvex and generally requires a
combinatorial search because choices interact when predictors are correlated.
The lasso replaces the discontinuous $\ell_0$ count by the convex relaxation
$\norm\beta_1$. Geometrically, an $\ell_2$ constraint is a smooth ball, whereas
the $\ell_1$ ball has corners on coordinate axes; an RSS contour often first
touches an $\ell_1$ corner, producing exact zeros.

\subsection{Orthonormal Design: Hard, Soft, and Ridge Shrinkage}

Suppose the response and predictors are centered and $X^\mathsf TX=I$. Define
$z=X^\mathsf Ty=\widehat\beta_{\mathrm{OLS}}$. Then
\[
 \frac12\norm{y-X\beta}^2
 =\text{constant}+\frac12\sum_{j=1}^p(\beta_j-z_j)^2,
\]
so each penalized problem separates coordinate by coordinate.

For best subset selection, coordinate $j$ compares the cost $z_j^2/2$ from
setting $b=0$ with the cost $\lambda_0$ from setting $b=z_j$. Therefore
\begin{equation}
 \widehat\beta_j^{\ell_0}
 =z_j\ind\{|z_j|>\sqrt{2\lambda_0}\},
 \label{eq:hard-threshold}
\end{equation}
which is \emph{hard thresholding}: retain the OLS coefficient unchanged or
delete it.

For the lasso, minimize
$q(b)=\tfrac12(b-z_j)^2+\lambda_1|b|$. Away from zero,
\[
 q'(b)=
 \begin{cases}
  b-z_j+\lambda_1,&b>0,\\
  b-z_j-\lambda_1,&b<0.
 \end{cases}
\]
At zero the subgradient condition is
$0\in-z_j+\lambda_1[-1,1]$, which holds exactly when
$|z_j|\leq\lambda_1$. Hence
\begin{equation}
 \widehat\beta_j^{\mathrm{lasso}}
 =\operatorname{sign}(z_j)(|z_j|-\lambda_1)_+,
 \label{eq:orthonormal-soft-threshold}
\end{equation}
the soft-thresholding rule. In contrast, ridge with objective
$\tfrac12\norm{y-X\beta}^2+\tfrac{\lambda_2}{2}\norm\beta^2$ gives
\begin{equation}
 \widehat\beta_j^{\mathrm{ridge}}=\frac{z_j}{1+\lambda_2}.
 \label{eq:orthonormal-ridge}
\end{equation}
Thus $\ell_0$ deletes or retains, lasso deletes or shrinks, and ridge shrinks
every coordinate continuously.

Exact support recovery needs conditions stronger than those needed for good
prediction, including sufficient signal and restrictions on correlations.
A sparse fitted model should not automatically be interpreted as identifying
the true causal variables.

The preferred penalty depends on signal structure. Lasso is often effective
when relatively few predictors carry signal, because setting irrelevant
coefficients to zero can substantially reduce variance. Ridge can perform
better when signal is dense or distributed across correlated groups, because
deleting coefficients creates unnecessary bias and lasso may select one
member of a correlated group arbitrarily. One-hot encoded levels and basis
expansions are examples where group-aware penalties may be preferable to
coordinatewise lasso.

\subsection{KKT}

Let $r=y-X\widehat\beta$. The Karush--Kuhn--Tucker conditions are
\begin{equation}
 X_j^\mathsf Tr=
 \begin{cases}
   \lambda\operatorname{sign}(\widehat\beta_j),
      &\widehat\beta_j\neq0,\\
   z_j,\quad\abs{z_j}\leq\lambda,
      &\widehat\beta_j=0.
 \end{cases}
 \label{eq:lasso-kkt}
\end{equation}
Thus a variable stays at zero when its correlation with the current residual
does not exceed $\lambda$. These conditions certify optimality and support
screening rules.

If $\lambda\geq\norm{X^\mathsf Ty}_\infty$, the all-zero vector is optimal
when the response and columns have been centered.

\subsection{Coordinate Descent}

Holding all other coefficients fixed, define the partial residual
$r_j=y-\sum_{k\neq j}X_k\beta_k$. The coordinate update is
\begin{equation}
 \beta_j\leftarrow
 \frac{S(X_j^\mathsf Tr_j,\lambda)}{\norm{X_j}^2},
 \qquad
 S(z,\lambda)=\operatorname{sign}(z)(\abs z-\lambda)_+.
 \label{eq:lasso-coordinate}
\end{equation}
The soft-thresholding operator sets small correlations exactly to zero.
Cyclic coordinate descent is efficient for sparse matrices. Warm starts along
a decreasing grid of $\lambda$ values greatly reduce computation; convergence
should be checked through KKT residuals or the primal--dual gap.

\section{Regression Splines and Basis Expansions}

Regularization reduces estimation variance within a chosen model class, but it
cannot remove approximation bias caused by an overly restrictive class. For
example, shrinking the coefficients of $\beta_0+\beta_1x$ still leaves a
straight line. A basis expansion instead writes
\[
 f(x)=\sum_{j=0}^M\beta_jh_j(x).
\]
For a fixed collection of basis functions, define $B_{ij}=h_j(x_i)$. Then
fitting remains ordinary linear regression:
\[
 \widehat\beta=(B^\mathsf TB)^{-1}B^\mathsf Ty
\]
when $B$ has full column rank. The fitting algorithm is unchanged; what has
changed is the linear space of candidate functions.

\subsection{Polynomial and Local Bases}

Polynomial regression uses $h_j(x)=x^j$:
\[
 f(x)=\beta_0+\beta_1x+\cdots+\beta_dx^d.
\]
It is nonlinear in $x$ but linear in its coefficients, and degree $d$ can be
chosen by CV. Increasing $d$ lowers approximation bias but raises estimation
variance and can make the fit unstable near boundaries. With $p$ predictors,
a complete polynomial expansion through total degree $d$ contains
\[
 M(p,d)=\binom{p+d}{d}
\]
terms including the intercept. Thus explicitly enumerating nonlinearities and
interactions quickly becomes combinatorial.

Step functions provide local constant fits by using interval indicators.
They are easy to interpret but discontinuous. A more useful local construction
uses the hinge function
\[
 (x-\xi)_+=\max(x-\xi,0).
\]
A linear spline with interior knots $\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_K$ has representation
\begin{equation}
 f(x)=\beta_0+\beta_1x+\sum_{k=1}^K\theta_k(x-\xi_k)_+.
 \label{eq:linear-spline-hinge}
\end{equation}
It is continuous, has $K+2$ coefficients, and its slope changes by $\theta_k$
after knot $\xi_k$.

\subsection{Cubic Regression Splines}

A degree-$d$ regression spline is piecewise polynomial with continuity
constraints at knots $\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_K$. A cubic spline requires the
function and its first two derivatives to agree across every knot. Counting
parameters makes its dimension transparent: $K+1$ cubic pieces have
$4(K+1)$ raw coefficients, while value, slope, and curvature matching impose
$3K$ constraints. Hence the spline space has dimension
\[
 4(K+1)-3K=K+4.
\]
The truncated-power basis
\[
 1,x,x^2,x^3,(x-\xi_1)_+^3,\ldots,(x-\xi_K)_+^3
\]
has exactly this size and automatically enforces the smoothness constraints.
More generally, a degree-$d$ truncated-power basis is
\[
 1,x,\ldots,x^d,(x-\xi_1)_+^d,\ldots,(x-\xi_K)_+^d.
\]

B-spline bases span the same spline space but have local support and much
better numerical conditioning. Each cubic B-spline basis function is nonzero
on at most four consecutive knot spans, so changing one coefficient while
holding the others fixed changes the represented function only locally.
This does \emph{not} mean that changing one observation and refitting has only
a local effect: least squares may change many coefficients, so the fitted
curve can move throughout the domain.

With a fixed basis matrix $B$, fitting is linear regression. Flexibility is
controlled by degree, knot locations, number of knots, or a roughness penalty.
Knots are often placed at empirical quantiles so data-rich regions receive
more resolution, while CV can select their number. Boundary behavior matters:
natural cubic splines impose $f''=0$ at both boundary knots and extend linearly
outside them. With $K$ interior knots their dimension is $K+2$, two fewer than
an unrestricted cubic spline, and their tail behavior is typically more stable
than that of a high-degree global polynomial.

Local support alone does not solve high-dimensional feature growth. If each of
$p$ predictors has $m$ basis functions, a full tensor-product basis has $m^p$
terms. Additive models avoid this explosion by excluding interactions unless
they are introduced deliberately.

\subsection{Smoothing Splines}

The cubic smoothing spline minimizes
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{i=1}^n\{y_i-f(x_i)\}^2
 +\lambda\int\{f''(t)\}^2\dd t
 \label{eq:smoothing-spline}
\end{equation}
over functions with square-integrable second derivative. The minimizer is a
natural cubic spline with knots at the distinct observed $x_i$ values. Thus an
apparently infinite-dimensional optimization has a finite-dimensional
solution.

For a suitable basis, the coefficient problem is
\[
 \widehat\beta=(B^\mathsf TB+\lambda\Omega)^{-1}B^\mathsf Ty,
 \qquad
 \Omega_{jk}=\int B_j''(t)B_k''(t)\dd t.
\]
The fitted values are $S_\lambda y$ and
$\operatorname{df}(\lambda)=\tr(S_\lambda)$. As $\lambda\to0$ the fit
interpolates; as $\lambda\to\infty$ it approaches a straight line.
Generalized cross-validation approximates leave-one-out error by
\[
 \operatorname{GCV}(\lambda)
 =\frac{n^{-1}\norm{(I-S_\lambda)y}^2}
        {\{1-\tr(S_\lambda)/n\}^2}.
\]

\subsection{GAMs}

A generalized additive model (GAM) assumes
\[
    g\{\E(Y\mid X=x)\}
    =\beta_0+\sum_{j=1}^pf_j(x_j),
\]
where $g$ is a link function. Each component is represented by a basis and
regularized with a smoothness penalty. Identifiability commonly uses
$\sum_i f_j(x_{ij})=0$.

GAMs capture nonlinear main effects while retaining component plots and
efficient fitting. Backfitting alternately updates each $f_j$ using partial
residuals. Penalized IRLS handles non-Gaussian responses. The additive
assumption excludes interactions unless terms such as tensor-product smooths
are explicitly included.

\section{Decision Trees}

A regression tree partitions feature space into terminal regions
$R_1,\ldots,R_M$ and predicts
\[
    \widehat f(x)=\sum_{m=1}^M\widehat c_m\ind\{x\in R_m\},
    \qquad
    \widehat c_m=\frac1{\abs{R_m}}\sum_{i:x_i\in R_m}y_i.
\]
At each node, CART greedily chooses a feature $j$ and threshold $s$ minimizing
the sum of squared errors in the two child nodes. For classification, common
node impurities are
\[
 Q_{\mathrm{Gini}}=1-\sum_k\widehat p_k^2,\qquad
 Q_{\mathrm{entropy}}=-\sum_k\widehat p_k\log\widehat p_k.
\]

Trees model interactions automatically, need little preprocessing, and are
invariant to monotone transformations of individual features. A single tree
is unstable: small data changes can alter early splits and the whole
partition. Greedy training is computationally feasible but does not find the
globally optimal tree.

Continuous split search can be done after sorting each feature. Missing
values require an explicit strategy, such as imputation, learned default
directions, or surrogate splits. Unrestricted high-cardinality categorical
features can receive an unfairly large chance to reduce training impurity.

\subsection{Pruning}

Grow a sufficiently large tree $T_0$, then choose a subtree by cost-complexity
pruning:
\begin{equation}
    C_\alpha(T)=\sum_{m=1}^{\abs T}
       \sum_{i:x_i\in R_m}(y_i-\widehat c_m)^2
       +\alpha\abs T.
    \label{eq:tree-pruning}
\end{equation}
Weakest-link pruning produces a nested sequence of optimal subtrees as
$\alpha$ increases. Cross-validation chooses $\alpha$; the one-standard-error
rule often yields a smaller, more stable tree.

Stopping growth early can miss a useful split whose benefit appears only
after another split. Growing then pruning separates search from complexity
selection.

\section{Random Forests}

Random forests average many deep randomized trees:
\[
    \widehat f_{\mathrm{RF}}(x)=\frac1B\sum_{b=1}^B\widehat f_b(x).
\]
Each tree uses a bootstrap sample, and each split considers a random subset of
features. Feature subsampling decorrelates trees; averaging then reduces
variance more effectively.

If identically distributed tree predictions have variance $\sigma^2$ and
pairwise correlation $\rho$, the average of $B$ trees has variance
\[
 \rho\sigma^2+\frac{1-\rho}{B}\sigma^2.
\]
Increasing $B$ removes only the second term. Forest performance therefore
depends on both individual tree strength and inter-tree correlation.

Out-of-bag predictions evaluate observation $i$ using only trees whose
bootstrap samples excluded it. They provide a convenient internal error
estimate, though an external test set is still needed after extensive tuning.

\subsection{Bagging}

Bagging fits the same unstable learner to bootstrap samples and averages:
\[
    \widehat f_{\mathrm{bag}}(x)
    =\frac1B\sum_{b=1}^B\widehat f^{*(b)}(x).
\]
For squared loss, averaging reduces variance. It helps trees because they are
unstable, but changes little for an already stable linear estimator.

Permutation importance measures the loss increase after shuffling a feature
in out-of-bag data. It measures predictive reliance, not causal effect, and
can split importance among correlated variables. Impurity-based importance is
biased toward continuous or high-cardinality features.

\section{Boosting as Functional Gradient Descent}

Boosting builds an additive model
\[
    F_M(x)=F_0(x)+\sum_{m=1}^M\nu\gamma_mh_m(x)
\]
by sequentially fitting weak learners. For differentiable empirical loss
$\sum_iL(y_i,F(x_i))$, iteration $m$ computes pseudo-residuals
\[
    r_{im}=-\left.
       \frac{\partial L(y_i,F)}{\partial F}
       \right|_{F=F_{m-1}(x_i)},
\]
fits $h_m$ to $(x_i,r_{im})$, chooses a step $\gamma_m$, and updates
$F_m=F_{m-1}+\nu\gamma_mh_m$. Thus boosting is gradient descent in function
space.

For squared loss, pseudo-residuals are ordinary residuals. For binary logistic
loss they are closely related to $y_i-\widehat p_i$. Tree depth controls
interaction order; learning rate $\nu$ and number of trees trade off. Smaller
$\nu$ usually requires more iterations and often generalizes better.

Boosting can overfit, especially with deep trees, noisy labels, or excessive
iterations. Tune on validation data and monitor training and validation loss.
Unlike bagging, trees are dependent and sequential: each corrects errors of
the current ensemble.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Gradient boosting}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State Initialize $F_0(x)=\argmin_c\sum_iL(y_i,c)$.
\For{$m=1,\ldots,M$}
  \State Compute $r_{im}=-\partial L(y_i,F)/\partial F$ at
  $F=F_{m-1}(x_i)$.
  \State Fit a weak learner $h_m$ to $\{(x_i,r_{im})\}$.
  \State Choose $\gamma_m$ by line search.
  \State Set $F_m=F_{m-1}+\nu\gamma_mh_m$.
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
\section{Maximum Margin Classifiers}

For labels $y_i\in\{-1,1\}$, a linear classifier predicts
$\operatorname{sign}(\beta_0+x^\mathsf T\beta)$. For separable data, the
hard-margin SVM minimizes $\norm\beta^2/2$ subject to
$y_i(\beta_0+x_i^\mathsf T\beta)\geq1$. Its geometric margin is
$1/\norm\beta$. Feature scaling matters because distance and the penalty
depend on units.

\subsection{Soft-Margin SVM}

Nonseparable data use slack variables:
\begin{equation}
 \min_{\beta_0,\beta,\xi}\frac12\norm\beta^2+C\sum_i\xi_i
 \quad\text{subject to}\quad
 y_i(\beta_0+x_i^\mathsf T\beta)\geq1-\xi_i,\quad \xi_i\geq0.
 \label{eq:soft-margin-primal}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, minimize $\norm\beta^2/2+
C\sum_i[1-y_i(\beta_0+x_i^\mathsf T\beta)]_+$. Large $C$ gives less
regularization; small $C$ permits violations for a wider margin.

\subsection{SVM Duality}

Eliminating the primal variables gives
\begin{equation}
 \max_{\alpha}\left\{\sum_i\alpha_i-\frac12\sum_{i,j}
 \alpha_i\alpha_jy_iy_jx_i^\mathsf Tx_j\right\}
 \quad\text{s.t.}\quad
 0\leq\alpha_i\leq C,\quad \sum_i\alpha_iy_i=0.
 \label{eq:svm-dual}
\end{equation}
The primal coefficient is
$\widehat\beta=\sum_i\widehat\alpha_i y_ix_i$. Convexity and feasibility give
strong duality.

\subsection{Support Vectors and Kernels}

KKT complementary slackness shows that observations with $\alpha_i=0$ do not
directly affect the boundary. Those with $\alpha_i>0$ are support vectors:
$0<\alpha_i<C$ indicates a point on the margin, while $\alpha_i=C$ indicates
a point inside the margin or misclassified.

A positive-semidefinite kernel
$K(x,z)=\inner{\phi(x)}{\phi(z)}$ implicitly maps inputs to a feature space.
Replacing inner products in \eqref{eq:svm-dual} gives
\[
 f(x)=\widehat\beta_0+\sum_i\widehat\alpha_i y_iK(x_i,x).
\]
Polynomial and Gaussian radial kernels are common. Kernel width and $C$ must
be tuned jointly. The Gram matrix costs $O(n^2)$ memory. The raw margin is not
a probability; calibrate it on held-out data if probabilities are required.

\section{PCA from Constrained Optimization}

For centered $X\in\R^{n\times p}$, the first principal loading solves
\begin{equation}
 v_1=\argmax_{\norm v=1}\Var(Xv)
 =\argmax_{\norm v=1}v^\mathsf TX^\mathsf TXv.
 \label{eq:pca-rayleigh}
\end{equation}
The Lagrange equation makes $v_1$ the leading covariance eigenvector.
Subsequent loadings maximize variance subject to orthogonality. If
$X=UDV^\mathsf T$, scores are $UD$, loadings are $V$, and component $j$
explains variance proportional to $d_j^2$. Standardize columns when their
scales are not comparable. PCA is unsupervised: high variance need not predict
$Y$.

\subsection{PCA as LoRA}

Here LoRA means low-rank approximation. The Eckart--Young--Mirsky theorem
states
\begin{equation}
 X_k=U_kD_kV_k^\mathsf T
 =\argmin_{\rank(A)\leq k}\norm{X-A}_F^2,\qquad
 \norm{X-X_k}_F^2=\sum_{j>k}d_j^2.
 \label{eq:eckart-young}
\end{equation}
Modern low-rank adaptation uses an update $\Delta W=AB^\mathsf T$. It shares
the representation idea, but its factors optimize a supervised objective
rather than arise from a data SVD.

\subsection{PCR connection}

Principal components regression regresses $y$ on the first $k$ scores:
\[
 \widehat\beta_{\mathrm{PCR},k}=V_kD_k^{-1}U_k^\mathsf Ty.
\]
PCR discards low-variance directions; ridge shrinks them continuously. Because
PCR selects directions without $y$, a low-variance component can be highly
predictive. Select $k$ by CV rather than explained variance alone.

\section{K-means}

$K$-means minimizes
\begin{equation}
 \min_{C_1,\ldots,C_K,\,\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_K}
 \sum_{k=1}^K\sum_{i\in C_k}\norm{x_i-\mu_k}^2.
 \label{eq:kmeans-objective}
\end{equation}
Lloyd's algorithm alternates nearest-center assignment with updating each
center to its cluster mean. The objective decreases, but convergence is only
to a local optimum. Use $K$-means++ and multiple initializations, standardize
differently scaled features, and handle empty clusters explicitly.

$K$-means favors roughly spherical, similarly sized clusters and is sensitive
to outliers. Choose $K$ using stability, substantive usefulness, silhouette
summaries, or a model-based criterion; no single diagnostic defines the
scientifically correct number.

\section{Hierarchical Clustering}

Agglomerative clustering starts with singleton clusters and repeatedly merges
the closest pair. Common linkages are
\begin{align*}
 d_{\mathrm{single}}(A,B)&=\min_{i\in A,j\in B}d(i,j),\\
 d_{\mathrm{complete}}(A,B)&=\max_{i\in A,j\in B}d(i,j),\\
 d_{\mathrm{average}}(A,B)&=\frac1{\abs A\abs B}
     \sum_{i\in A,j\in B}d(i,j).
\end{align*}
Ward linkage chooses the smallest increase in within-cluster sum of squares.

The dendrogram records merge heights; branch order within a merge is not
meaningful. Single linkage can recover elongated clusters but chains through
nearby points. Complete linkage favors compact clusters. Distance, scaling,
and preprocessing define similarity. A dendrogram always displays structure,
even for homogeneous noise, so assess stability by perturbation or bootstrap
co-clustering and require substantive validation.
